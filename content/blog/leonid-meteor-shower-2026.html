The Leonids have a reputation. In 1833 they turned the sky into a snowstorm of fire, with witnesses reporting more than 100,000 meteors an hour. Most years they are much calmer. But even a calm Leonid night brings fast, bright meteors, and in 2026 the Moon gets out of the way for the best part of the night.

This guide gives you the dates, the peak time in your time zone, a Moon-aware plan, and a clear picture of what you will really see.

Peak Time in Your Time Zone

The International Meteor Organization expects the shower's regular maximum on November 17 at about 23:45 UT. That moment arrives at very different clock times around the world, and it decides which night you should watch.

WherePeak moment (23:45 UT)Best time to watch
London (GMT)Nov 17, 11:45 pmNight of Nov 17–18, after the Moon sets
Paris, Berlin, Tunis (UTC+1)Nov 18, 12:45 amNight of Nov 17–18, midnight to dawn
New York (EST)Nov 17, 6:45 pmPre-dawn Nov 17 and Nov 18
Chicago (CST)Nov 17, 5:45 pmPre-dawn Nov 17 and Nov 18
Los Angeles (PST)Nov 17, 3:45 pmPre-dawn Nov 17 and Nov 18

What this means: if you live in the Americas, the exact peak falls in the evening or afternoon, when Leo is still below the horizon. Your best views come in the hours before dawn, when the radiant is high. Rates are usually decent on both sides of the peak. If you live in Europe or North Africa, the peak lands in the middle of the night, which is ideal.

The Moon on Peak Night

Moon trouble is the usual story for meteor showers, and the Orionids earlier this season had it. For the Leonids, the news is better than it first looks.

  • The Moon reaches first quarter on November 17 at 11:48 UT, about 45% lit that night, according to MeteorShowerCal.
  • A first-quarter Moon is high in the evening and sets around midnight. TimeAndDate lists moonset in New York at 11:55 pm on November 17. In-The-Sky.org gives 00:38 for Washington, DC. The Royal Museums Greenwich says the Moon sets just before midnight in the UK.
  • The Leonid radiant rises late (about 10:54 pm in New York, per TimeAndDate) and climbs until dawn.
Heads up: EarthSky points out that a first-quarter Moon can drown out the fainter meteors earlier in the night. Plan for the dark hours after moonset, not the early evening. Some websites describe the Moon differently, so check a moonset time for your own city.

A Simple Viewing Plan

  1. Pick two mornings, not one. Try November 17 and November 18 before dawn. Cloud is the biggest risk. TimeAndDate notes that November 17–18 has been cloudy 61% of the time in New York since 2000.
  2. Check your moonset time. Plan to be outside from about an hour after moonset until the sky starts to brighten.
  3. Get away from lights. A park, a field or a dark beach beats a lit driveway. Give your eyes 20 to 30 minutes to adjust.
  4. Lie back and look wide. Meteors appear all over the sky. You do not need to stare at Leo, though it helps to face the east or southeast after midnight.
  5. Dress for the cold. November nights chill fast. A sleeping bag or thick blanket matters more than any gear.
  6. Stay at least an hour. Meteors come in clumps and gaps. At 10–15 per hour, you may see none for ten minutes and three in a minute.

Why the Leonids Are Famous: Meteor Storms

A meteor storm is a shower that produces more than 1,000 meteors an hour. Most astronomers use that threshold, according to EarthSky. The Leonids are one of very few showers to do it, and they have done it several times.

YearWhat happened
1833The first recorded modern meteor storm. Witnesses reported over 100,000 meteors an hour. It is known as "the night the stars fell."
1866–1867Another storm, roughly 33 years later.
1899A storm was predicted and did not appear. EarthSky notes many astronomers saw it as a blow to public trust in astronomy.
1966Observers in the southwestern US saw 40 to 50 meteors per second for about 15 minutes on the morning of November 17.
1999–2002Strong displays and, in 2001, a storm with thousands of meteors an hour over North America and Hawaii.
2026No storm expected. Normal rates of about 10–15 an hour.

Sources: EarthSky "Leonid meteor shower: All you need to know in 2026"; American Meteor Society.

The storms line up with the comet's visits. The next close approach of the comet is in 2031, so the Leonids may get livelier in the early 2030s. Predictions are made by modeling the dust trails, and the models have improved. Still, nobody can promise a storm years ahead.

Comet Tempel-Tuttle, the Source

Every Leonid is a grain of dust shed by Comet 55P/Tempel-Tuttle. It was found by Wilhelm Tempel on December 19, 1865, and independently by Horace Tuttle on January 5, 1866. It circles the Sun about once every 33 years, which explains the storm cycle. The International Meteor Organization lists its next perihelion as May 20, 2031.

The grains are tiny, often no bigger than a grain of sand. They hit the upper atmosphere at about 70 km/s. At that speed they glow for a split second, and the fastest ones leave glowing trains that can linger for a moment. Many Leonids look green or white. Occasional brighter ones, called fireballs, are worth reporting to the American Meteor Society.

Five Tips for a Better Meteor Night

  • Skip the telescope. Your eyes see the widest part of the sky. Binoculars and telescopes shrink the field too much.
  • Turn your phone brightness to the lowest setting or use a red-light mode. White light ruins your night vision for several minutes.
  • Bring something warm to drink. It keeps you outside longer, and longer is better.
  • Take photos the easy way. Put your phone on a tripod, use night mode, and shoot a time-lapse or 10 to 15 second exposures toward Leo.
  • Look at the other showers too. The Northern Taurids peak around November 9 and the Southern Taurids around November 5, both with low rates but occasional fireballs. They are good practice nights. See our Orionid meteor shower 2026 guide for the shower before this one.

What a normal Leonid hour looks like

Imagine you lie out for an hour at 3 a.m. with a clear, dark sky. You might see ten or twelve meteors. They will not arrive evenly. Expect a quiet five or ten minutes, then two close together. Most will be short, white and quick. One or two may be bright enough to cast a faint shadow or leave a thin glowing trail. If you see a really brilliant one, you have likely seen a fireball.

If you only have a short window, early morning on November 18 and November 17 gives you the best chance. If clouds block both, the shower stays active for days afterward at lower rates, so a clear night on November 19 or 20 can still reward you.

Simple pack list

  • A sleeping bag or two blankets
  • A hat and gloves, since November nights are cold
  • A red flashlight (or a phone in red-light mode)
  • A hot drink and a snack
  • A weather app and a moon-phase app for your location

FAQ

When is the Leonid meteor shower in 2026?

It is active from November 6 to 30, 2026, and peaks on the night of November 17–18.

What time is best to watch the Leonids?

After midnight until dawn. The Moon is at first quarter and sets around midnight in many places, which leaves the early-morning hours dark.

How many Leonid meteors will I see?

About 10 to 15 an hour under dark skies at the peak. From a town, you will see fewer.

Will there be a Leonid meteor storm in 2026?

No. Scientists do not expect a storm this year. The parent comet is not due back until 2031.

Which direction should I look?

Look at a wide dark part of the sky. The meteors seem to come from Leo, which rises around midnight in the east and is highest at dawn, but they can appear anywhere.

Why are the Leonids so fast?

Their grains meet Earth almost head-on, at about 70 km/s. That speed makes many Leonids bright, with glowing trains.
