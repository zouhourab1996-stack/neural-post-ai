Why an eclipse is rare on any given night

A lunar eclipse needs a full moon and the Moon crossing Earth's shadow, so on most nights the answer to "is there a lunar eclipse tonight" is no.

What has to line up

During an eclipse, Earth sits between the Sun and the Moon, and its shadow falls on the lunar surface. That can only happen at full moon. The Moon's orbit is tilted about 5 degrees against Earth's path around the Sun, so most months the full moon slips above or below the shadow.

Eclipses cluster in two seasons a year, which is why you only get a few lunar eclipses annually. A quick filter follows. If the Moon tonight is a crescent, a quarter or a gibbous, no lunar eclipse is possible, so look at the phase first.

A lunar eclipse needs a full moon and a shadow crossing, so most nights have neither.

The three kinds of lunar eclipse

How deep the Moon goes into Earth's shadow decides what you will see.

Type What happens What you see
Total The whole Moon enters the darkest shadow (umbra) A dark Moon glowing orange or red
Partial Part of the Moon enters the umbra A dark bite taken from the disc
Penumbral The Moon passes only through the faint outer shadow A slight dimming that is easy to miss

Why the Moon can look orange without an eclipse

A full moon near the horizon often looks orange or gold. Its light crosses more atmosphere there, which scatters blue light away. That is not an eclipse. A real one shows a shadow creeping across the disc, not an evenly tinted Moon. During totality, the red color comes from sunlight filtered through Earth's atmosphere, as NASA explains.

Step 2. Convert the times to your time zone

Take the UTC time from the calendar and subtract your zone's offset. That gives your local time, and the date often moves back a day.

Use the right UTC offset

Pick the offset that applies on the eclipse date. August eclipses fall in daylight time, and February eclipses fall in standard time.

Time zone Standard time Daylight time
Eastern UTC-5 UTC-4
Central UTC-6 UTC-5
Mountain UTC-7 UTC-6
Pacific UTC-8 UTC-7
Alaska UTC-9 UTC-8
Hawaii UTC-10 No daylight time

Arizona stays on Mountain standard time all year, so it uses UTC-7 even in summer.

Work one example through

Maximum for the August 2026 eclipse was 04:12 UTC on August 28. Subtract the daylight offset for each zone and the date flips back to August 27 everywhere west of the Eastern zone.

Zone Calculation Local maximum
Eastern 04:12 minus 4 hours 12:12 a.m., Aug. 28
Central 04:12 minus 5 hours 11:12 p.m., Aug. 27
Mountain 04:12 minus 6 hours 10:12 p.m., Aug. 27
Pacific 04:12 minus 7 hours 9:12 p.m., Aug. 27

Different sources round to the minute differently, so allow a few minutes either side. Start watching at least 30 minutes before the time listed for maximum, because you want to see the Moon enter the shadow, not arrive after the show.

Step 3. Confirm the Moon is up and the sky is clear

A lunar eclipse is visible from anywhere the Moon is above the horizon, so your only questions are whether the Moon is up at your location and whether clouds will block it.

Check moonrise and your horizon

A full moon rises close to sunset, so an early-evening eclipse can begin while the Moon is still low. For the February 2027 eclipse, the Moon rises with the event already underway across much of North America, and it is not visible from the West Coast. In the same way, parts of the West caught the August 2026 eclipse in progress at moonrise.

Search your city's moonrise time, then find a spot with an open view to the east or southeast. A hill, a parking lot roof or a park beats a street between buildings.

Plan around clouds and light

Clouds matter more than city lights. A partial or total eclipse is bright enough to see from a suburb, though darker skies bring out the red color and help with faint penumbral dimming. Check your hourly cloud forecast on weather.gov a few hours ahead and again just before you head out.

If clouds are forecast, drive toward clearer sky if you can do it safely, or fall back on a livestream. Observatories and space agencies often stream eclipses.

Clouds ruin more eclipses than city lights do, so check the forecast twice.

Step 4. Watch the eclipse and know what you see

Go outside, look up, and let your eyes adjust. A lunar eclipse is completely safe to view without glasses, and the Moon changes slowly, so patience beats equipment.

What to bring

Use your eyes first. Binoculars add detail on a partial or total eclipse, and a tripod keeps a phone or camera steady for night-mode shots. A jacket and a chair are the most useful items on the list.

  • Find your moonrise time and an open eastern horizon
  • Check the cloud forecast
  • Bring binoculars and a camera tripod if you have them
  • Arrive 30 minutes before the shadow starts

What happens at each stage

The stages unfold in a predictable order, and knowing them keeps you from giving up early.

Stage What to look for
Penumbral begins Almost nothing. The Moon dims slightly after a while.
Partial begins A dark bite on the Moon's left edge as seen from the Northern Hemisphere.
Maximum The deepest shadow. A thin bright sliver in a partial eclipse, or full color in a total one.
Partial ends The bite shrinks from the left edge and the Moon brightens.

A penumbral eclipse skips the dramatic part. Compare the Moon at the start and at maximum, because the dimming is gradual.

Watch for the shadow's edge, not the color. The bite is the proof it is an eclipse.
