The nights of **December 26–28, 2026** offer an easy-to-follow skywatching event: the bright, waning gibbous Moon will pass through the same region of the sky as **Jupiter, Mars and Regulus**, the brightest star in Leo. Together, these objects will make a noticeable arc or loose grouping—not a perfectly straight planetary line, and not a physical alignment in space.

You do not need a telescope to enjoy the scene. The Moon, Jupiter, Mars and Regulus are visible to the unaided eye when they are above your horizon and the sky is clear. The Moon's movement from night to night is the key: it appears close to Jupiter and Regulus on December 27, then shifts toward Mars on December 28.

:::tldr At a glance
- **Main observing dates:** December 26–28, 2026.
- **Best nights:** December 26–27 and December 27–28, with the grouping changing shape each night.
- **Where to look:** The eastern sky after the objects rise; the group is in the constellation Leo.
- **What to spot:** The bright Moon, brilliant Jupiter, reddish Mars and blue-white Regulus.
- **Equipment:** Your eyes are enough. Binoculars can add detail, but are not necessary.
- **Important:** Exact rise times and directions depend on your location, horizon and local daylight-saving rules.
:::

## What is happening in the sky on December 26–28?

The Moon travels eastward along its orbit and changes its apparent position against the background stars each night. During these dates, it passes through Leo, where Jupiter, Mars and Regulus are also visible.

This creates a temporary visual grouping. The objects are not close together in space: they only appear in the same area of our sky from Earth's point of view. Jupiter and Mars are planets within our solar system, while Regulus is a distant star.

The sequence is especially useful for casual observers because the four objects are bright enough to identify without specialist equipment. Jupiter is the brightest planet in the grouping, Mars has a reddish appearance, and Regulus is the bright star marking the heart of Leo.

## December 26–27: the Moon joins Jupiter and Regulus

On the night of **December 26–27**, look toward the eastern sky after the Moon and planets have risen. The nearly full-looking waning Moon will appear in Leo alongside Jupiter, Regulus and Mars, forming a broad arc.

The exact arrangement depends on the time and your location. In the Northern Hemisphere, the grouping is expected to be easiest to follow as it climbs higher during the night. Observers in the Southern Hemisphere can also see the event, although the group appears in a different part of their sky.

Do not worry if the objects do not look perfectly lined up. Their apparent spacing changes as the Moon moves, and the sky's orientation differs with latitude.

## December 27: the Moon passes close to Jupiter and Regulus

**December 27 is the most useful date for identifying the close pairings.** The Moon will be about 82% illuminated and will appear close to brilliant Jupiter in the constellation Leo. Published event predictions put their apparent separation at roughly **1.4 degrees**. The Moon will also appear close to Regulus, at a similar apparent distance.

These are close approaches on the sky, not collisions or close physical encounters. Jupiter remains hundreds of millions of kilometres away, while Regulus is many light-years from Earth.

Jupiter is easy to distinguish because it shines steadily and is much brighter than the surrounding stars. Regulus appears as a blue-white point. If you are unsure which object is which, use a planetarium app set to your exact location and date rather than relying only on colour.

## December 28: the Moon shifts toward Mars

On **December 28**, the Moon will have moved farther east against the stars and will appear near reddish Mars. The predicted apparent separation is about **4.7 degrees**.

Mars is not as bright as Jupiter in this grouping, but its warm colour can help you pick it out. The Moon and Mars should both be visible to the naked eye when they are above the horizon and not hidden by clouds or buildings.

The change between December 27 and 28 is a simple way to see the Moon's orbital motion for yourself. Take a photo from roughly the same place and at a similar time on both nights, and compare the Moon's position relative to Jupiter, Mars and Regulus.

## Best time to watch: use your local sky, not a universal clock

There is no single best clock time for every country. The Moon and planets rise and set at different times depending on longitude, latitude and the date. Buildings, hills, haze and cloud cover can also block the view.

Use this practical plan:

1. Check a location-specific sky chart for the evening of December 26, 27 or 28.
2. Find Leo and confirm the positions of Jupiter, Mars and Regulus before going outside.
3. Choose a place with a clear view toward the eastern horizon.
4. Begin observing once the Moon and the objects you want to see have risen high enough to clear nearby buildings or trees.
5. Return the following night to see how the Moon has shifted.

The event is a multi-night observing opportunity, not a brief flash that happens at one exact minute. If clouds spoil one evening, try the next.

## Can you see the December 2026 Moon and planet grouping without a telescope?

Yes. **The naked eye is the best starting point.** The Moon, Jupiter, Mars and Regulus are bright enough to be seen without binoculars or a telescope under clear conditions.

Binoculars can help you explore the surrounding star field, but the Moon is bright and can make nearby faint stars harder to see. A telescope is not required to enjoy the broad grouping, and a wide view is often more satisfying than high magnification.

For a simple observing session:
- Let your eyes adjust to the darkness for around 15–20 minutes.
- Avoid bright phone screens; use a dim red setting if your sky-chart app offers one.
- Keep a clear, wide view of the sky rather than looking through a narrow gap.
- If you use binoculars, never point them at the Sun.

## Photographing the Moon, Jupiter and Mars

A phone camera can record the overall scene, although the Moon may appear much brighter than the planets. For a better result, try these steps:

- Stabilise your phone on a tripod or firm surface.
- Use the standard or wide camera rather than extreme digital zoom.
- Tap the Moon on the screen and lower exposure so its bright surface is not completely washed out.
- Take one frame that includes the surrounding sky, then a separate closer frame of the Moon.
- Repeat from the same location on December 27 and 28 to document the Moon's movement.

A camera may not capture the same colours and contrast that your eyes perceive. That is normal: phone processing, exposure and local light pollution all affect the final image.

## Is this a planetary alignment?

It is more accurate to call this an **apparent sky grouping** or a series of lunar conjunctions and close approaches. The Moon, Jupiter and Mars do not form a straight line in three-dimensional space. They only appear near one another when projected against the distant background of the sky as seen from Earth.

This distinction matters because popular descriptions sometimes use “alignment” for any group of bright objects. In astronomy, the word can describe an apparent arrangement, but it should not be mistaken for the planets physically lining up in space.

## How this event fits into December's night sky

The December grouping is one of several opportunities to observe the sky before the year ends. Earlier in the month, the Geminid meteor shower reaches its annual peak on December 13–14. The December solstice follows on December 21, and the year's final full Moon occurs on December 24.

## Frequently asked questions

### When can I see the Moon near Jupiter and Mars in December 2026?

The main observing dates are December 26–28, 2026. The Moon appears close to Jupiter and Regulus on December 27, then shifts toward Mars on December 28.

### What time should I look?

Look after the Moon and planets have risen above your local horizon. Exact rise times vary by location, so use a sky chart configured for your town or city.

### Can I see the grouping without binoculars?

Yes. The Moon, Jupiter, Mars and Regulus are visible to the naked eye under clear skies. Binoculars are optional.

### What is the bright star near Jupiter?

The bright star in the grouping is Regulus, the brightest star in the constellation Leo. Jupiter is a planet and will generally appear much brighter.

### Is the Moon actually close to Jupiter and Mars?

They appear close together from our viewpoint on Earth, but they are separated by enormous distances in space. This is an apparent alignment, not a physical meeting.

### Will the event be visible worldwide?

The grouping can be observed from both hemispheres, but its height and direction vary with latitude and local time. A clear horizon and local sky chart will help you plan.

### Do I need a telescope to photograph the event?

No. A phone or camera with a stable support can capture the broad scene. A telescope is not needed for the naked-eye grouping.
