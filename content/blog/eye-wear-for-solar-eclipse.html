The only safe eye wear for a solar eclipse is a pair of eclipse glasses or a handheld solar viewer that meets the ISO 12312-2 standard and comes from a manufacturer you can trace. Regular sunglasses, even very dark ones, are not safe. Neither is smoked glass, a stack of sunglasses, or a phone screen. If you cannot get proper glasses in time, use a pinhole projector and look at the shadow, never at the Sun.

The confusion comes from the packaging. Almost every pair sold online prints "ISO certified" on the frame, and anyone can print that. Shoppers end up comparing colors, cartoon prints and pack sizes, when the only questions that matter are who made the filter and whether it is in one piece. Brand names are copied, and a counterfeit pair can look identical to a real one.

This guide covers what safe eclipse eye wear is, where to buy it, how to test a pair at home, how to use it on the day, and which upcoming eclipses you need it for. Prophetic computes eclipse dates and times to the minute from ephemeris data, and we treat astrology as a symbolic, reflective practice, not a prediction engine. Eye safety is not symbolic, so every rule below comes from NASA and the American Astronomical Society.

1. What counts as safe eye wear for a solar eclipse

Safe solar eye wear blocks almost all visible sunlight plus the invisible ultraviolet and infrared light that burns the retina. It does this with a special filter film, not a tinted lens.

The ISO 12312-2 standard

ISO 12312-2 is the international standard for filters used to look directly at the Sun. NASA describes safe solar viewers as thousands of times darker than ordinary sunglasses. The standard covers how much light gets through, how evenly the filter blocks it, and the quality of the material.

The catch is that you cannot confirm the standard by reading the label. As the AAS Solar Eclipse Task Force puts it, the only real proof is lab testing by an accredited lab, and that is the manufacturer's job, not yours.

"ISO 12312-2" printed on a frame is a claim, not a test result.

Types of safe solar eye wear

You have more than one option, and each suits a different viewer.

Type What it is Best for
Paper or plastic eclipse glasses Filter film in a card or plastic frame you wear like glasses Most people, families, groups
Handheld solar viewer A card with a filter window you hold up to your eyes Children and people who wear prescription glasses
Clip-on solar viewers Filters that clip onto a cap or your own frames Long viewing sessions outdoors

What is never safe

These fail every time, and some of them feel safe because the Sun looks dim through them.

  • Regular or polarized sunglasses, at any darkness
  • Several pairs of sunglasses stacked together
  • Smoked glass, exposed film negatives, or CDs
  • Photographic neutral density filters not made for the Sun
  • Looking at the Sun on a phone screen while pointing the phone at it without a solar filter on the lens
  • Eclipse glasses that are torn, scratched, punctured, or coming loose from the frame

A dim view does not mean a safe view. Your retina has no pain receptors, so damage can happen without any warning.

2. Where to buy eclipse glasses you can trust

Buy from a manufacturer on a vetted list, or from a dealer that manufacturer names publicly. That single rule removes most of the risk.

Use the AAS list of manufacturers

The American Astronomical Society keeps a list of manufacturers of safe solar viewers and filters whose products have been tested to ISO 12312-2. It includes long-running makers such as American Paper Optics, Rainbow Symphony, Baader Planetarium, Lunt Solar Systems and Thousand Oaks Optical, among others.

The AAS no longer vets every reseller. Its advice is simple.

  1. Buy directly from a manufacturer on the list.
  2. Or buy from an authorized dealer that the manufacturer itself identifies.
  3. Skip random marketplace listings and shops that are "cashing in" on an eclipse.

Seeing a listed brand name on the package is not enough. Some sellers print a trusted maker's name on their own products.

Buying in Europe and North Africa

The next total eclipse, on August 2, 2027, crosses Spain, Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, Libya and Egypt. If you buy in Europe, the Spanish volunteer group OVAE runs its own vetted supplier list called Eclipse Seguro, which the AAS points to for European buyers. The AAS notes that it does not supervise that list, so treat it as a second reference, not a guarantee.

When a free pair is fine

Glasses handed out by an astronomy club, a university physics department, a science museum or a planetarium are almost always safe. Those groups buy from sources they know. Glasses from an unknown online ad are the ones to question.

Trace the filter to its maker. If you cannot, treat the glasses as unsafe.

3. How to choose and inspect solar viewers

There is no reliable at-home test that can certify eclipse glasses as safe. Do not look at the Sun to test a pair, even briefly. A filter can appear dark enough to the eye while still transmitting dangerous ultraviolet or infrared radiation.

Choose a product that conforms to ISO 12312-2 and buy from a manufacturer or authorised reseller listed by the American Astronomical Society's solar-viewer safety resources. A printed ISO claim alone is not proof that a product was properly tested; the AAS list identifies suppliers whose products have been assessed by accredited laboratories.

Before every use, inspect both sides of the filter. Do not use it if it is scratched, punctured, torn, wrinkled, loose from the frame or otherwise damaged. Follow the manufacturer's instructions and supervise children using solar viewers.

NASA and the AAS both state that ordinary sunglasses, no matter how dark, are not safe for direct solar viewing. If you cannot verify the source and condition of a viewer, do not use it; use an indirect projection method instead.

Sources: NASA eclipse eye-safety guidance and AAS guidance on verifying solar viewers.

4. How to wear eclipse glasses on the day

Good glasses still need good habits. Most eclipse eye injuries happen through mistakes in use, not through bad filters.

The basic routine

Follow the same order every time you look up.

  1. Stand still and look down at the ground.
  2. Put the glasses on, covering both eyes fully.
  3. Look up at the Sun.
  4. Look away from the Sun before you take the glasses off.
  5. Supervise children the whole time, and use a handheld viewer if their glasses do not fit snugly.

With prescription glasses

Keep your normal glasses on and wear the eclipse glasses over them, or hold a handheld viewer in front of them. Do not swap one for the other while facing the Sun.

Never combine them with optics

This is the most dangerous mistake. Do not look through binoculars, a telescope or a camera viewfinder while wearing eclipse glasses. NASA warns that concentrated sunlight from the optics will burn straight through the filter and into your eye.

Binoculars, telescopes and camera lenses need their own solar filter mounted on the front, the end pointing at the Sun. Any filter that screws into the eyepiece, where your eye goes, is unsafe and should be thrown away. If you have never used solar optics, get help from an experienced observer first.

Total eclipse: the one exception

During a total eclipse, there is a short window called totality when the Moon fully covers the Sun's bright face. Only then can you take the glasses off and look directly. You will know it is time when you can no longer see any part of the Sun through your glasses.

The moment a sliver of bright Sun returns, put the glasses back on. During a partial or annular eclipse there is no totality at all, so the glasses stay on the whole time.

Glasses on for every partial phase. Off only for totality, and back on at the first flash of sunlight.

5. No glasses? Use indirect viewing

If you cannot get trusted eye wear in time, you do not have to miss the eclipse. Indirect viewing lets you watch the Sun's shape without looking at it.

Pinhole projection

Stand with the Sun at your back and let sunlight pass through a small hole onto a surface in front of you. Watch the projected image, never the Sun itself.

  • Index card: punch a small hole and hold the card above a sheet of white paper.
  • Colander: each hole throws its own little crescent Sun onto the ground.
  • Your hands: cross your slightly open fingers into a waffle shape and look at the shadow.
  • A leafy tree: the gaps between leaves dapple the ground with crescents during the partial phases.

A box projector

For a sharper image, tape aluminum foil over a hole at one end of a cardboard box, punch a pinhole in the foil, and tape white paper inside the far end. With the Sun behind you, look into the box through a second hole cut in its side. NASA publishes this design on its eclipse safety page.

Pinhole projection does not work during totality, because the eclipsed Sun is too dim to project. That is fine, since totality is the one time you can look directly.

Why the annular eclipse needs extra care

An annular eclipse leaves a bright ring of Sun around the Moon. The sky never gets fully dark, and the ring is still bright enough to injure your eyes. Keep your glasses on from start to finish, even at the moment of the "ring of fire."

Lunar eclipses are different

You need no eye wear for a lunar eclipse. The Moon is only reflecting sunlight, so it is safe to watch with bare eyes or binoculars. Prophetic's guide to checking for a lunar eclipse tonight explains how to find one and when to look.

Frequently asked questions

Can I reuse eclipse glasses from a past eclipse?

Yes, if they came from a trusted maker and the filter is undamaged. Run the three-step test and check for scratches or pinholes first. If you are unsure where they came from, replace them.

Are polarized sunglasses safe for an eclipse?

No. Polarized lenses cut glare, not the intensity of direct sunlight. They let through far too much light and offer no protection when you look at the Sun.

Can I take photos through my eclipse glasses?

Holding glasses over a phone lens gives poor results and tempts you to look at the screen in the Sun's direction for too long. Use a phone solar filter made for the job, and keep your own glasses on while you frame the shot.

How long can I look through eclipse glasses?

Properly made, undamaged glasses are designed for direct viewing of the Sun. If you have any doubt about their source, limit yourself to a few seconds every few minutes.

Protect your eyes, then enjoy the sky

Safe eye wear for a solar eclipse comes down to three checks. Is it ISO 12312-2 eye wear from a maker you can trace? Does it pass the indoor, outdoor and one-second tests? Is the filter free of damage? If the answer to any of these is no, switch to pinhole projection.

Order your glasses early for the February and August 2027 eclipses, keep them flat in an envelope, and test them again a week before the date. Then you can spend the eclipse looking up, not worrying.
