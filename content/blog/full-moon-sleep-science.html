Every month, someone in your orbit announces they couldn't sleep — "must be the full moon." Every month, someone else rolls their eyes. The eye-rollers have the better of most full-moon lore. On sleep, though, they're arguing against two peer-reviewed studies, and those studies side with your insomniac friend more than either camp expects.

Here's the honest version: what the studies actually measured, the detail nearly every article gets wrong (the worst nights aren't full-moon night), the explanations that survived the data and the one that didn't — and a way to test the whole thing on yourself this October, for free, in thirty seconds a night.

Is full-moon insomnia real?

"Real" is doing a lot of work in that question, so take the direct answer first: two independent lines of evidence found a measurable, modest change in human sleep around the full moon. One study followed people through real life — villages, towns, a university campus. The other locked volunteers in windowless sleep labs. Both landed on the same shape.

What neither found is drama. This isn't werewolf territory — it's bedtime sliding later and deep sleep getting shallower, measured in minutes. If you don't track your sleep, you might feel it as restlessness, unusually vivid dreams, or waking up less refreshed. If you sleep like a rock, you may notice nothing at all.

One more thing said plainly: the wider research on the lunar effect is genuinely mixed. Several analyses before these two found nothing, and the Basel team behind the lab study called the earlier work inconclusive. Two well-run studies make a pattern worth respecting — not a law of nature.

The 2021 field study: 562 sleep records, one pattern

In January 2021, a team led by University of Washington biologist Leandro Casiraghi published a study in Science Advances that did what lunar-effect research rarely does: it tracked ordinary people living ordinary lives — up to two months each, with wrist sleep trackers — across radically different light environments.

The field arm covered 98 people in three Toba/Qom communities in Argentina, ranging from a community with full access to electricity to one with none at all. The second arm re-analyzed sleep data from 464 University of Washington students in Seattle — electric lights, indoor evenings, no particular reason to look at the sky.

The pattern repeated everywhere, with one twist nobody expects. The sleep loss didn't peak on full-moon night — it peaked three to five nights before it. In that window, people fell asleep 30 to 80 minutes later than their norm and slept 20 to 90 minutes less. The effect was strongest in the community without electricity, weaker in Seattle — but still there.

Why before? In the evenings leading up to a full moon, a bright, nearly-full moon is already above the horizon in the hours after sunset — moonlight arriving exactly when your brain is deciding whether the day is over. After the full moon, moonrise slides later and later into the night, missing your bedtime entirely. The study's own logic points at evening light, not at the full moon as a single magical moment.

The 2013 lab study: no windows, no moonlight, still less sleep

If evening light were the whole story, this next study should have found nothing. It's the reason the full-moon sleep question stayed alive.

In July 2013, chronobiologist Christian Cajochen and his colleagues at the University of Basel reported in Current Biology what happened when they went back through earlier sleep-lab recordings and sorted them by moon phase. The 33 volunteers had slept in windowless, tightly controlled rooms — no moonlight, no clocks, no time cues. And crucially, neither the volunteers nor the investigators had known, back when the data was collected, that the moon would ever be a variable.

Around the full moon, those volunteers took about five minutes longer to fall asleep, slept about twenty minutes less, and — the striking number — showed a 30% drop in the EEG brain activity that marks deep sleep. Their melatonin levels ran lower, and they rated their sleep as poorer.

"The lunar cycle seems to influence human sleep," Cajochen summed it up, "even when one does not 'see' the Moon and is not aware of the actual moon phase." A study that small can't carry the whole claim alone, and retrospective analysis has real limits. But eight years later the 2021 field data landed on the same shape — and that pairing is exactly why this question deserves a fair hearing.

Read the two columns as one story: light makes the effect bigger — much bigger — but something persists after you strip the light away. That residue is the puzzle the three explanations below fight over.

Why it happens: light, an inner clock, or belief?

1. Evening moonlight — the strongest candidate. It fits everything the field study found: the timing (evenings before the peak), the gradient (strongest with no electric light, weakest in a lit-up city), the fade-out after full moon. Before artificial light, moonlight was the only evening light humanity had, and staying up on bright nights may simply be a very old habit. This explanation covers most of the measured effect. It just can't touch the windowless lab.

2. A built-in "circalunar" rhythm — the honest hypothesis. To account for the lab data, researchers proposed that humans may carry a roughly 29.5-day internal rhythm synchronized to the moon — a lunar cousin of the circadian rhythm. It's an appealing idea, and so far it is only an idea: no one has located such a clock or explained how it would run. The lab data is the hint. The clock is the guess.

3. Gravity and "the water in your body" — rejected by the data. You'll hear that the moon pulls the tides and we're mostly water. Two problems. The moon's gravitational pull on something the size of a person is vanishingly small — tides work on oceans because oceans are enormous. And the moon's pull peaks at the new moon just as it does at the full, roughly every 14.75 days. The 2021 study checked the new moon: no sleep effect. If gravity ran your sleep, both halves of the month would suffer.

And a fourth factor the eye-rollers correctly raise: expectation. Bracing for a bad night is a proven way to get one. But the 2013 volunteers didn't know the moon was a variable — so belief can't be the whole story either.

The honest summary: something real happens to sleep around the full moon. Evening moonlight explains most of it, an internal lunar clock might explain the rest, gravity is almost certainly innocent — and expectation rides on top of whatever else is true.

The eight-night self-test

You will not out-sample a 562-person study. But you're the only sleeper whose nights you can actually feel, and the protocol is trivial:

  1. Pick the window: October 20–27. Eight nights, covering baseline, peak and fade.
  2. Log three numbers a night: the time you turned the lights off, the time you woke, and a 1–10 restedness score within ten minutes of waking. A phone note is enough.
  3. Keep your curtains exactly as they normally are. The point is to measure your real life, not a laboratory version of it.
  4. Optional upgrade: repeat the whole thing November 3–9, the week into the new moon — your personal control group. The 2021 study predicts the difference should lean toward October.

One caveat, stated plainly: you are a sample of one, for one cycle, and knowing which week you're in can color your restedness score. If the pattern shows up anyway, you've caught the lunar effect as it happens. If it doesn't, you've learned your sleep doesn't care — which is also worth knowing before you blame the next moon.

FAQ

Why can't I sleep during a full moon?

The best-evidenced answer: evening moonlight. In the three to five nights before a full moon, a bright moon is already up during your evening, delaying sleep — the 2021 Science Advances study measured 30–80 minutes later sleep onset and 20–90 minutes less sleep on those nights. A 2013 lab study suggests something may persist with no moonlight at all, possibly an internal rhythm, but that part remains a hypothesis.

How much sleep do you lose on a full moon?

In the field data, people lost 20 to 90 minutes on the worst nights — three to five nights before the full moon, not the night of the peak itself. In the controlled lab study, volunteers slept about 20 minutes less and lost 30% of deep-sleep brain activity around the full moon. The effect varies a lot between people; some notice nothing.

Does the moon affect sleep even with blackout curtains?

Partly. The strongest measured factor is evening light, so blocking moonlight in your bedroom should blunt most of the effect. But the 2013 study found the pattern in windowless rooms with zero moonlight, so light isn't the whole mechanism — an internal lunar rhythm is the leading hypothesis, and it is still unproven.
