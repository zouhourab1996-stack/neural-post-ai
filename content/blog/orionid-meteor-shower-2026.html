Key Takeaways

  • The Orionid meteor shower peaks on the night of October 21–22, 2026. It's active from October 2 to November 7.
  • Expect about 10–20 meteors an hour under dark skies at the peak. Fewer from towns and cities.
  • The Moon is the problem this year. A bright waxing gibbous Moon, roughly three-quarters lit, washes out the evening sky. Full moon follows on October 26.
  • Best plan: watch in the dark gap after moonset and before dawn on the mornings of October 21, 22 and 23.
  • Every Orionid is a speck of Halley's Comet, hitting the air at about 66 km/s (41 miles per second).

Some meteor showers are loud. The Orionids are quieter, but they carry a story no other October event can match. Each streak you catch is dust shed by Halley's Comet, the most famous comet in history, burning up overhead.

In 2026 there's a catch: a bright Moon sits in the sky on peak night. This guide shows you exactly when to go out so the Moon has already set, what to expect, and how to make the most of a few dark hours before sunrise.

What time is the peak where you live?

The Moon Problem, and How to Beat It

The American Meteor Society describes the 2026 Moon as a waxing gibbous phase, leaving dark skies "only available for a few hours between moonset and dawn." The Royal Observatory Greenwich and the BBC give the same warning.

A waxing Moon rises in the afternoon and sets after midnight. That's good news for early risers. Once it drops below the horizon, the sky gets dark just as the Orionid radiant climbs highest.

This year, the alarm clock beats the late night. Go to bed early, and step outside after the Moon has set. — Prophetic Editorial Team

How to find your moonset time

  1. Open a moonrise and moonset table for your city, for example on timeanddate.com.
  2. Find the moonset time for the early hours of October 22.
  3. Plan to be outside from moonset until the sky starts to brighten, about an hour before sunrise.
Good to know: Even with moonlight, the brightest Orionids still show. The BBC notes that brighter meteors should remain visible, especially from darker places. So if you can't get up at 4 a.m., a look after midnight is still worth it, just with lower counts.

Where to Look, and Where You Live

Orionids seem to fly out of a point in the constellation Orion, called the radiant. But you don't need to stare at Orion. Meteors cross the whole sky. In fact, the ones farther from the radiant often look longer and more dramatic.

Northern Hemisphere (US, Canada, Europe, North Africa)

  • Orion rises in the east late in the evening and is high in the south before dawn.
  • NASA suggests lying flat on your back with your feet toward the southeast, then taking in as much sky as you can.

Southern Hemisphere (Australia, South Africa, South America)

  • The shower is still visible, but the radiant sits lower in the north.
  • NASA suggests feet toward the northeast. Expect somewhat fewer meteors than viewers farther north.

A Simple Viewing Plan for October 21–23

The Orionids have a broad, plateau-like maximum, so you get three good chances. Pick the clearest one.

MorningWhy try it
Wednesday, October 21 (pre-dawn)Rates already near peak; Moon sets a little earlier
Thursday, October 22 (pre-dawn)The classic peak morning for most of the world
Friday, October 23 (pre-dawn)Still strong activity; good backup if clouds win

Your 6-step checklist

  1. Check the weather the evening before. Clouds beat every other factor.
  2. Find a dark spot away from street lights, with a wide view of the sky.
  3. Bring a reclining chair or blanket, warm layers and a hot drink. October nights get cold.
  4. Give your eyes 20–30 minutes to adjust. NASA says that's how long dark adaptation takes.
  5. Put the phone away, or use red-light mode. One glance at a bright screen resets your night vision.
  6. Stay at least an hour. Meteors come in clumps, with quiet gaps in between.
Safety first: Tell someone where you're going if you drive to a remote dark-sky spot, park off the road, and never look at the rising Sun through binoculars or a telescope.

Why the Orionids Are Special: Halley's Comet

Every time Halley's Comet swings close to the Sun, it sheds ice and dust. Over centuries, that debris has spread along its orbit. Earth crosses the stream twice a year: in May, giving the Eta Aquariids, and in October, giving the Orionids.

Halley itself last visited in 1986. The BBC notes it won't be visible from Earth again until 2061. Until then, the Orionids are the closest most of us will get.

Fast and bright

At about 66 kilometers per second, Orionids are among the fastest meteors of the year. Speed brings energy, so they often leave glowing "trains" that linger for a second or two after the streak.

Some years surprise

The American Meteor Society notes that a normal year brings 10–20 meteors an hour at the peak, but from 2006 to 2009 the rates reached 50–75 an hour, on par with the Perseids. Recent years have been average. No outburst is predicted for 2026, but the Orionids have surprised observers before.

You won't see Halley's Comet again until 2061. But this month, you can watch it fall through the sky one grain at a time. — Prophetic Editorial Team

Spiritual Meaning, the Honest Version

Meteors have carried meaning for as long as people have looked up. Wishing on a "shooting star" is one of the oldest folk customs in the world. Here's how we like to read the Orionids, with both feet on the ground.

A message from the past

The dust in this shower left Halley's Comet on earlier passes, long before any of us were born. Symbolically, that makes the Orionids a beautiful prompt for reflecting on what you've inherited: habits, stories and strengths passed down to you.

A threshold moment in the sky

In 2026 the Orionid peak sits right before a busy run in the astrology calendar:

  • October 24: Mercury stations retrograde in Scorpio. Our Mercury retrograde in Scorpio guide covers all 12 signs.
  • October 26: the Full Hunter's Moon in Taurus. Read our Hunter's Moon 2026 guide.
  • Ongoing: Venus is retrograde until mid-November. See Venus retrograde 2026.

Many astrologers treat the days before a retrograde as a time to wrap up, review and release. A dark pre-dawn hour under falling stars is a lovely setting for exactly that.

A 10-minute meteor reflection

  1. Settle in and let your eyes adjust in silence.
  2. Think of one thing you want to let go of before the retrograde begins.
  3. With the next meteor you see, silently name it and let it go.
  4. With the one after that, name one thing you want to carry forward.
  5. Write both down when you get home.
Our honest note: Meteors are grains of comet dust burning up in the upper atmosphere. There's no scientific evidence that they affect your life or luck. The value of a ritual like this is the quiet, the focus and the intention you set, and that is real enough.

Photographing the Orionids With a Phone

Catching a meteor on camera takes patience, but modern phones can do it.

  • Use a tripod or prop the phone on something steady.
  • Turn on night mode with the longest exposure your phone allows.
  • Aim about 45° away from Orion, toward the darkest part of the sky.
  • Shoot continuously with an interval or time-lapse app. Most frames will be empty, and that's normal.
  • Keep the Moon out of the frame, or wait until it has set.

Five Mistakes That Ruin a Meteor Night

  1. Going out too early. In 2026 the evening sky is moonlit. The best hours come after moonset.
  2. Giving up after ten minutes. Meteors arrive in bursts. A quiet spell is normal, so give it at least an hour.
  3. Staring only at Orion. Meteors near the radiant look short. Scan the wider sky to catch long, bright streaks.
  4. Checking your phone. Every bright screen costs you several minutes of night vision.
  5. Dressing for the afternoon. Pre-dawn hours in late October are cold, even in mild climates. Bring a hat, gloves and a blanket.

Missed the peak because of clouds? Don't worry. The shower stays active into early November, and the slower, sometimes bright Taurid meteors are also active this time of year, so a clear dark morning can still reward you.

FAQ

When is the Orionid meteor shower 2026?

It's active from October 2 to November 7, 2026, and peaks on the night of October 21–22.

What time should I watch the Orionids in 2026?

In the hours after moonset and before dawn on October 21, 22 and 23. A bright waxing gibbous Moon brightens the sky earlier in the night.

How many meteors will I see?

Up to about 10–20 an hour under dark, moon-free skies at the peak. From towns or with moonlight, expect fewer.

Which direction should I look?

Look at a wide, dark part of the sky rather than straight at Orion. In the Northern Hemisphere, NASA suggests lying back with your feet toward the southeast.

Are the Orionids from Halley's Comet?

Yes. The Orionids come from dust left by Comet 1P/Halley, which also creates the Eta Aquariid shower in May.

Do I need a telescope?

No. Meteors move too fast and cover too much sky. Your eyes are the best tool.
