Uranus reaches **opposition on Wednesday, November 25, 2026**. This is the point in its yearly observing cycle when Earth lies approximately between Uranus and the Sun, placing the distant planet opposite the Sun in our sky. Uranus is therefore above the horizon for much of the night and is near its brightest and largest apparent size for the year.

The event is a good reason to look toward Taurus after darkness, but expectations matter: Uranus is faint. Under a very dark sky, an experienced observer with excellent eyesight may glimpse it without optical aid; binoculars or a small telescope make the search much more realistic. The nearly full Moon around this date adds glare, so choose a spot with a clear view and keep the Moon outside your field of view.

:::tldr At a glance
- **Date:** November 25, 2026.
- **Opposition time:** approximately 22:41 UTC (23:41 in Tunisia).
- **Constellation:** Taurus.
- **Brightness:** around magnitude 5.6.
- **Best equipment:** binoculars or a telescope; naked-eye visibility is difficult and depends on exceptionally dark skies.
- **Important limitation:** opposition does not mean Uranus will look large or colourful to the unaided eye.

## What time is Uranus at opposition?

The listed opposition moment is approximately **22:41 UTC on November 25**. That corresponds to **23:41 in Tunisia (UTC+1)**. The exact instant is useful for calendars, but you do not need to observe at that minute. Uranus remains a night-sky target over the surrounding evenings, and the practical observing window depends on your location, horizon and weather.

Opposition is a geometric alignment, not a sudden visible flash. The planet does not change dramatically from one minute to the next. Its advantage is that it rises around sunset, reaches its highest point around local midnight, and sets around sunrise, while its distance from Earth is close to its yearly minimum.

For the event date and the wider November sky, compare the observing notes from [Star Walk's November 2026 sky guide](https://starwalk.space/en/news/night-sky-tonight) and [timeanddate's November skywatching guide](https://www.timeanddate.com/news/astronomy/night-sky-november-2026).

## Where to find Uranus

Uranus is in the constellation **Taurus**. It is not a bright, obvious planet like Venus or Jupiter. At roughly magnitude 5.6, it sits close to the theoretical naked-eye limit under dark conditions, and light pollution or moonlight can make it disappear from view.

Use this simple search plan:

1. Find a dark location with an unobstructed view of the eastern sky as the evening progresses.
2. Use a reliable star chart or planetarium app set to your exact location and date.
3. Identify Taurus and its familiar star fields before pointing binoculars at the predicted position.
4. Keep the bright Moon outside the binocular field if possible.
5. If you are unsure which point is Uranus, compare the field with a current chart rather than guessing from colour alone.

Do not expect a dramatic blue disc through ordinary binoculars. Uranus usually looks star-like at low magnification. A telescope at suitable magnification can reveal a tiny disk, but atmospheric steadiness and the instrument's optics matter.

## Why opposition is the best time of year

At opposition, Earth is on the same side of the Sun as the planet. The planet appears opposite the Sun in the sky, so it is visible for most of the night. It is also generally closest to Earth for that year's orbit and consequently appears at its brightest.

That does not make Uranus easy to see. It is far beyond Saturn and Jupiter, and even at opposition it remains a faint target. A pair of binoculars, a stable mount and a chart are more useful than magnification alone. Start with a wide field, confirm the star pattern, and only then move to a telescope if you have one.

The [Planetary Society's 2026 space-events calendar](https://www.planetary.org/articles/calendar-of-space-events-2026) also lists the November 25 opposition and explains why the date is a useful observing opportunity.

## The Moon is the main observing obstacle

The November full Moon occurred on November 24, so the sky around opposition is still bright with moonlight. This can wash out the faint stars used to navigate toward Uranus. If the Moon is high, try observing later when it is lower, move behind a building or other safe obstruction to block its glare, or wait for a darker night later in the week.

A few practical details improve your chances:

- Let your eyes adapt to darkness for 20–30 minutes.
- Avoid looking at a bright phone screen; use a dim red display if your chart app supports it.
- Choose a site away from streetlights and illuminated buildings.
- Check cloud cover and local atmospheric conditions before travelling.
- Never point binoculars or a telescope at the Sun.

## What else is happening in the sky this week?

Uranus is not the only reason to watch the sky at the end of November:

- **November 25–26:** the Moon appears near Aldebaran and Betelgeuse, creating a broad triangular pattern with two prominent stars.
- **November 26:** Mars appears close to Regulus in Leo. Both are visible to the naked eye when they are above the horizon.
- **November 27:** Venus reaches a particularly bright morning-sky appearance for 2026. Look toward the eastern sky before sunrise.
- **November 27–28:** the November Orionids are expected to reach their modest annual peak. Rates are much lower than the famous August Perseids or December Geminids, and moonlight and local sky conditions affect what you see.

These dates are observing guides, not guarantees of visibility from every location. A planet can be above the horizon while hidden by buildings, haze or clouds. For local rise and set times, use a location-aware sky chart rather than applying one city's schedule to the whole world.

## Uranus opposition in astrology: symbolism, not a forecast

In modern astrology, Uranus is associated with disruption, independence, invention and the urge to question established patterns. An opposition is traditionally interpreted as a moment of contrast: something previously kept in the background may become easier to notice when it is reflected back through another person, situation or choice.

That is a symbolic reading, not an astronomical effect on human behaviour. The physical opposition is a measurable alignment in the solar system; astrological meanings belong to a cultural and interpretive tradition and have not been established as scientific predictions.

For a reflective exercise, consider one area where you want more freedom and one responsibility that still deserves care. Instead of making a sudden decision because of a transit, write down the change you want, the evidence behind it and one small, reversible step you can take.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I see Uranus with the naked eye on November 25, 2026?

Possibly under exceptionally dark, clear skies and with excellent eyesight, because its brightness is near the naked-eye limit. Most observers will find binoculars or a telescope much more reliable, especially with the bright Moon nearby.

### Is Uranus visible all night at opposition?

It is above the horizon for most of the night, rising around sunset and setting around sunrise. The exact times depend on your location.

### Does Uranus look blue through binoculars?

Usually not as a clear, colourful disk. At low magnification it generally looks like a faint point. A telescope may show a tiny disk under suitable conditions.

### Is the opposition time the only time I can observe it?

No. Opposition marks the centre of the best annual observing period, not a one-minute event. The nights around November 25 are also suitable if the weather and sky conditions cooperate.

### Does Uranus opposition cause sudden changes in people's lives?

There is no scientific evidence that a planetary opposition causes personal events. Astrology offers symbolic interpretations, but it should not replace evidence, careful judgement or professional advice.
