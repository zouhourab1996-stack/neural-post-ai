The short version

  • The solar eclipse of August 2, 2027 is a total solar eclipse. The Moon will completely cover the Sun for observers inside the narrow path of totality.
  • Totality crosses parts of southern Spain, Gibraltar, Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, Libya, Egypt, Saudi Arabia, Yemen, and Somalia. Other parts of Europe, Africa, Asia, and the Atlantic region see a partial eclipse.
  • NASA lists the greatest eclipse at 10:07 UT, with a central duration of about 6 minutes 23 seconds, a path width of about 258 km, and the greatest-eclipse point near 25.5°N, 33.2°E.
  • The maximum totality is near Luxor, Egypt. The exact local time depends on your city, so use a location-specific calculator rather than converting the global maximum time by guesswork.
  • Before and after totality, use certified solar viewers or eclipse glasses. Ordinary sunglasses are not safe.

The next major total solar eclipse is already on the calendar: Monday, August 2, 2027. It will not be a total eclipse everywhere. The Moon's darkest shadow will trace a narrow route from the Atlantic across southern Europe, North Africa, and the Middle East, while a much wider region sees only part of the Sun covered.

When is the solar eclipse in 2027?

The total solar eclipse occurs on August 2, 2027. The date is the same worldwide, but the local clock time changes as the shadow moves across the Earth. NASA's 2027 eclipse catalogue gives the greatest eclipse at about 10:07 Universal Time (UT). UT is the reference time used on the NASA page; it is not the local time shown on a travel itinerary.

There are five eclipses in 2027: an annular solar eclipse on February 6, penumbral lunar eclipses on February 20, July 18, and August 17, and the total solar eclipse on August 2. The August event is the one that produces totality from land along its central path.

2027 solar eclipse path and countries

The path of totality is the narrow corridor where the Moon fully covers the Sun. On August 2, 2027, the corridor crosses the Atlantic and then moves through or near:

  • Europe: southern Spain and Gibraltar.
  • North Africa: Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, Libya, and Egypt.
  • Middle East and nearby regions: Saudi Arabia, Yemen, and parts of the surrounding region.
  • East Africa and the Indian Ocean: Somalia and ocean areas farther along the track.

The National Solar Observatory lists cities and regions under the total path including Cádiz, Tangier, Gibraltar, Benghazi, Luxor, and Jeddah. Being in the same country does not guarantee totality: the path is narrow, and a city outside it may see only a partial eclipse. Check the exact town on an interactive path map before booking travel.

Outside the totality corridor, the eclipse can still be visible as a partial solar eclipse across a much wider area, including much of Europe, Africa, parts of Asia, the Atlantic, and the Indian Ocean. A partial eclipse is a different viewing experience: the Sun is never completely covered, so the same strict eye-safety rules apply throughout the event.

How long will totality last?

NASA gives a central duration of approximately 6 minutes 23 seconds and a path width of approximately 258 kilometres. The National Solar Observatory identifies the area near Luxor, Egypt, as a location near the maximum duration, at roughly 6 minutes 22 seconds on its map.

Duration changes from place to place. Near the centreline, totality lasts longer. Near the northern or southern edge of the path, it lasts for less time. A location can therefore be inside the total path but have a very different experience from a city a few hundred kilometres away.

“Longest” does not mean “best” by itself. Cloud cover, horizon conditions, transport, crowding, and the ability to reach the exact centreline matter just as much. A shorter, clear observation can be better than a longer one hidden by cloud.

Where is the best place to see it?

The practical answer is: choose a location inside the path of totality with a good weather outlook, a clear horizon, and a realistic transport plan. The route includes many attractive observation areas, but the best choice depends on your departure point and how much flexibility you have.

  • Egypt: Luxor is close to the maximum-duration region and has a strong historical and tourism infrastructure, but demand may be high.
  • Southern Spain and Gibraltar: easier access for many European travellers, though coastal weather and local crowding should be checked closer to the date.
  • Morocco and North Africa: several locations fall under the path, but exact town-level timing and transport conditions must be verified.
  • Saudi Arabia and Yemen: some locations see totality, but travel, entry, local conditions, and safety planning require particular care.

Use the NASA eclipse catalogue for the astronomical reference and the Timeanddate path map for city-level planning. Treat travel articles as planning aids, not substitutes for local official information.

How to watch the August 2, 2027 eclipse safely

Looking at the uneclipsed or partially eclipsed Sun can injure your eyes. The only time direct viewing is safe without a solar filter is during the brief period of totality, when the Moon completely covers the bright solar disk. The instant the bright photosphere reappears, eye protection must go back on.

  • Use eclipse glasses or a handheld solar viewer that meets the relevant safety standard and is designed for direct solar viewing.
  • Inspect the viewer before use. Do not use it if the filter is scratched, punctured, torn, or otherwise damaged.
  • Do not substitute ordinary sunglasses, smoked glass, film, CDs, or homemade filters.
  • Follow the instructions for cameras, binoculars, and telescopes. Optical equipment needs a proper front-mounted solar filter; eclipse glasses alone do not protect an unfiltered camera or telescope.
  • Children should be supervised. It is easy to remove protection too early when the light changes dramatically.

The National Solar Observatory's eclipse-safety guidance explains the distinction between totality and the partial phases. Read it before buying equipment.

What the 2027 solar eclipse can mean in astrology

An eclipse has an astronomical definition first: it is an alignment of the Sun, Moon, and Earth. Astrology adds a symbolic layer. If you work with a natal chart, you can use the August 2 event as a date for observation and reflection rather than as a guarantee that a particular event will happen.

Start with the exact time and place of the eclipse in your chart software. Then note which house and natal planets are involved, using a consistent house system. A responsible interpretation asks: what area of life is already changing, what information is becoming visible, and what would I like to observe over the following weeks? It does not promise a breakup, a promotion, a move, or a financial outcome.

FAQ

Is the solar eclipse on August 2, 2027 total everywhere?

No. Totality is visible only inside the narrow path of totality. A much larger region sees a partial eclipse, and some places see no eclipse at all.

Will the 2027 eclipse be visible from the United States?

Not as a total solar eclipse. The global path is concentrated across the Atlantic, southern Europe, North Africa, the Middle East, and nearby regions. Check a location-specific map for the exact visibility from any US city.

What time is the 2027 solar eclipse?

The global maximum is about 10:07 UT, but your local time depends on your location. The first partial phase begins globally at 07:30:11 UT and the last partial phase ends at 12:43:10 UT. Use a city-level calculator for the useful local schedule.

How long is the 2027 total solar eclipse?

Maximum totality is about 6 minutes 23 seconds according to NASA's catalogue. The duration is shorter away from the centreline and changes from location to location.

Can I look at the eclipse through my phone camera?

Do not point an unprotected phone camera directly at the Sun for an extended period. Follow the phone or camera manufacturer's guidance and use proper solar-filter equipment. Eye protection and camera protection are separate issues.
