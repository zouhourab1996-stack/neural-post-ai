The Quadrantids are the meteor shower people forget, and then regret forgetting. They arrive in the first days of January, when everyone is tired, it is freezing and the sky is usually cloudy. Then the peak lasts a few hours instead of a couple of days. Get the timing right and it is among the richest showers of the year. Get it wrong and you stand in a field watching a handful of stragglers.

So here is the short answer for 2027. The Quadrantid meteor shower is active from December 28, 2026 to January 12, 2027, and the predicted maximum is January 4 at 03:25 UTC. For most of Europe and North Africa that falls in the dark, small hours of Monday morning. For North America it falls on Sunday evening, when the radiant is almost on the horizon. Below you'll find the local time in twelve cities, what the sky will actually deliver where you live, and, because this is an astrology site, a plain reading of the sky it lands in.

Which Hours to Pick Where You Live

This is where the Quadrantids are different from the Geminids or Perseids. You cannot just "go out on the peak night" and be fine. The hours matter.

  • Europe and North Africa (London, Paris, Tunis): you are the lucky ones. The maximum lands between 3:25 and 4:25 a.m., with the radiant 40° or more above the north-eastern horizon. Be outside by 2 a.m. and stay until twilight. If you can only do one hour, do 3 to 4 a.m.
  • Egypt and the Eastern Mediterranean: the predicted maximum, 5:25 a.m. in Cairo, is almost at the start of twilight. Aim for 3:30 to 5 a.m., the two hours leading into the peak, with the radiant already 40° to 50° high.
  • Gulf and India: the maximum arrives after sunrise. Your best dark hours finish between 1.5 and 3 hours before it. That is the rising side of the peak, which can still be rewarding, but you are not at the top.
  • East Asia: you get the dark hours more than seven hours before the maximum. Expect a modest, early-shoulder show, not the real thing.
  • The Americas: the peak falls in your evening, when the radiant is on or below the horizon (4° in New York, below the horizon in Los Angeles). The first dark hours after the peak are poor for the same reason. Your good geometry comes before dawn on January 4, with the radiant 45° to 60° up, but that is 7 to 10 hours after the maximum. It is worth going out, just don't expect Europe's numbers.
  • Southern Hemisphere: NASA says the shower can be seen from latitudes as far south as 51°S, but in practice from Sydney the radiant never clears the horizon during the night. Skip it.

One more thing about timing: the IMO says the predicted peak carries an uncertainty of about 0.1° of solar longitude, roughly 2.5 hours. If the real maximum lands a little early, Cairo and the Gulf win. A little late, Europe and the Americas pick up some of it. That is one more reason to stay out longer than you think you need to.

You may also see a different time elsewhere. EarthSky, for example, gives the predicted peak as 05:47 UTC, about two and a half hours later than the IMO's 03:25. Both fit inside the uncertainty. If you are in Egypt, the Gulf or India, the later time is good news. In western Europe the two answers just mean "stay out until twilight."

How Many Meteors Will You Really See?

The "ZHR 80" you see everywhere is a zenithal hourly rate: what one observer would count with the radiant straight overhead under a perfect sky. Nobody watches in those conditions. The IMO's own correction uses two things: how high the radiant is and how faint a star you can see (your limiting magnitude). With the Quadrantids' population index of 2.1 at maximum, this is what the formula gives for ZHR 80:

Calculated as ZHR 80 × sin(radiant altitude) ÷ 2.1^(6.5 − limiting magnitude). These are the figures at the maximum itself. Rates drop quickly away from it, and clouds or haze lower them further.

Two readings of that table are worth spelling out. At the London peak moment, with the radiant 44° up, a dark site gives about 38 an hour. That is roughly half of the headline number, and it is a good night. For the Americas, I can only offer a what-if. At the ceiling, a dark New York site at 4:30 a.m. could show about 47 an hour, but that is 7 hours after the maximum. If the rate has fallen to a third of the peak by then, you would see about 16 an hour. If it has fallen to a fifth, about 10. I'm not going to pretend I know the real fall-off, because nobody does for a given year.

That uncertainty is real, and it is the Quadrantids' signature. Look at how much the measured peak has moved over the years (visual maxima compiled by the IMO and listed on Wikipedia):

The IMO adds that in 2022, 2023 and 2025 the highest visual ZHR sat in the lower part of the 60 to 200 range, and in 2024 and 2026 it was close to the long-term average. There is a small reason for hope in 2027. The IMO notes that Jérémie Vaubaillon's modelling puts the stream slightly shifted towards the Sun this year, which might mean enhanced activity. It is a "might", and I wouldn't plan a night around it. I'd plan around the Moon, which for once is out of the way. The IMO calls the moonlight conditions perfect.

Even NASA's page lists two numbers: a fact box saying to expect fewer than 10 an hour under dark skies, and a paragraph saying 60 to 200 an hour under perfect conditions. The big number needs everything at once: the short peak, a high radiant and a truly dark sky.

Where to Look

Find the Big Dipper. In January it stands on its handle in the north-east in the middle of the night, and it climbs through the early morning. The Quadrantid radiant sits just beyond the end of the handle, in the northern corner of Boötes, close to the head of Draco. NASA's advice is practical: lie flat on your back with your feet pointing north-east and take in as much sky as you can.

Do not stare at the radiant. Meteors close to it have short trails and are easy to miss. Look 40° to 60° away from it, at the darkest piece of sky you have, and let your eyes relax. In the north-east, a Quadrantid slides in a long line across the sky. Allow at least 20 minutes for your eyes to adapt, and keep your phone dimmed or on red.

Why the Peak Is So Short

Most showers last about two days at their best. The Quadrantids last hours. NASA's explanation is that the stream is thin and Earth crosses it at a steep, nearly perpendicular angle, so we cut through it quickly. Wikipedia puts the time above half the maximum rate at around eight hours, while the IMO's radio data suggest it is wider than the traditional four-hour figure. The honest answer is "a few hours, depending on how you measure."

The parent is an oddity too. Asteroid 2003 EH1, found in March 2003 by the LONEOS survey, is about 3 km across and orbits the Sun every 5.52 years. Peter Jenniskens linked it to the shower in 2003, and it may be the remains of a comet seen from China, Japan and Korea around the year 1490. Wikipedia notes that the narrow stream suggests the material was shed within the last 500 years or so. The Quadrantids are also known for bright fireballs, brighter than magnitude −3 according to NASA, and the IMO notes that fireballs have been seen even a few days after the peak.

The name is a historical accident. In 1795 the French astronomer Jérôme Lalande drew a small constellation called Quadrans Muralis, the wall quadrant, between Boötes and Draco. The shower was first reported in January 1825 by Antonio Brucalassi in Italy, and in 1839 Adolphe Quetelet in Belgium and Edward Herrick in Connecticut each suggested it came back every year. The IAU dropped Quadrans Muralis in 1922, but the shower kept the name. You can still see the old constellation in the engraving above, with the instrument itself drawn at the top left.

The Cloud Problem

Winter is the other enemy. According to timeanddate.com, January 3–4 has been cloudy in New York about 72% of the time since the year 2000. That is one city, not a global statistic, but it matches what most northern observers will tell you. In 2020 NOAA published the satellite picture above with the headline that stormy weather had stopped most North Americans from seeing the shower.

My practical rule: the peak is too short to wait for a clearer night, as you could with the Geminids. But check the forecasts for January 2–3 and 4–5 as well. Rates at the shoulders are lower, not zero, and a clear sky on the wrong night beats a cloudy sky on the right one.

My Viewing Plan

Here's the plan I'd follow. Adjust the clock times with the table above.

  1. Two days before: look at cloud forecasts for Sunday night and Monday night. Decide on a location with a clear view to the north-east, with a hill or a building not blocking it.
  2. Dress for the cold you didn't expect: boots, two pairs of socks, a hat, a sleeping bag or a foam mat. A hot drink in a flask. January at 4 a.m. is colder than your forecast says because you are lying still.
  3. Arrive 30 minutes before your window starts, so your eyes have time to adapt.
  4. Europe and North Africa: be in position by 2 a.m., watch until twilight. The hour on either side of the predicted maximum is the best.
  5. The Americas: forget the evening of January 3 and set an alarm for about 3:30 a.m. local time on Monday. Go out for the last dark ninety minutes, when the radiant is high and the Moon is only just rising.
  6. Count, if you like. Note the start time, the meteors you see in each 15 minutes and how much sky was clear. It makes the next one easier to plan.

The Astrology of the 2027 Quadrantids

A short honest note before the symbolism. A meteor shower is not a planet. It doesn't occupy a degree in your chart, and the constellation Boötes is not a zodiac sign. The shower is a dated, shared sky event, and it is easy to attach an intention to. What I can say for certain is what the rest of the sky is doing at the IMO peak time, computed for 03:25 UTC on January 4, 2027:

  • Sun at 13°32′ Capricorn. The Sun entered Capricorn at the solstice on December 21, so this is the quiet, practical start of Capricorn season. (See our winter solstice 2026 guide for the start of it.)
  • Moon at 3°14′ Sagittarius, 12% lit. It entered Sagittarius around 20:58 UTC on January 3 and moves into Capricorn around 09:18 UTC on January 6. The full moon was the Cold Moon on December 24 (our Cold Moon guide), so this is the final stretch of that cycle, and the new moon in Capricorn follows on January 7 at 20:24 UTC, at 17°18′.
  • Mercury at 15° Capricorn, just past its conjunction with the Sun on January 1, so it is out of sight and out of the way. Good for quiet planning more than for announcements.
  • Mars at 10° Virgo, about six days from turning retrograde on January 10. (Details in our Mars retrograde 2027 guide.)
  • Jupiter at 26° Leo, retrograde since December 13 until April 13. See the Jupiter retrograde guide.
  • Saturn at 8° Aries and Venus at 27° Scorpio.

Here is my reading, and it is a reading, not a forecast. The Quadrantids reward preparation more than any other shower. You can't wander out and trust to luck, because the window is short and the weather is bad. That sits oddly well with a Capricorn Sun and a dark waning Moon: this is a sky for finishing the plan you drew up in December, not for starting ten new ones. Mars hesitating before its retrograde in Virgo asks the same thing in a practical way. Check the details, fix what you can, and don't commit to a big new push until the new moon on January 7.

If you like a simple ritual: before you go out, write one commitment for January on a small piece of paper, one you can actually do in the next 30 days. Don't make it a big resolution. Fold it. Each time you see a meteor, count it. When you have seen five, put the paper in your pocket and keep it until the new moon.

FAQ

When is the Quadrantid meteor shower in 2027?

It is active from December 28, 2026 to January 12, 2027. The IMO predicts the maximum on January 4 at 03:25 UTC, which is the night of January 3–4 in most of the world.

What time is the Quadrantid peak in my city?

Convert 03:25 UTC: 3:25 a.m. in London, 4:25 a.m. in Paris and Tunis, 5:25 a.m. in Cairo, 7:25 a.m. in Dubai, and 10:25 p.m. on January 3 in New York and Toronto. The exact peak can be off by about 2.5 hours either way.

Will the Moon interfere with the 2027 Quadrantids?

No. The Moon is a 12% waning crescent that rises between roughly 4 and 6 a.m. in most northern cities, and the new moon is on January 7. The IMO describes the moonlight conditions as perfect.

How many Quadrantids can I see per hour?

The ZHR is about 80, with a range of 60 to 200. In practice, a very dark European site at the peak moment gives roughly 35 to 45 an hour. A suburb gives 15 to 20, and a city under 10. Rates fall quickly away from the peak.

Can I see the Quadrantids from the United States?

Yes, but the timing is awkward. The peak falls in the evening of January 3, when the radiant is almost on the horizon. The best view is before dawn on January 4, with the radiant 45° to 60° high, though that is 7 to 10 hours after the predicted maximum.

Can I see the Quadrantids from the Southern Hemisphere?

Barely. NASA says the shower can be seen as far south as latitude 51°S, but from Sydney the radiant never rises during the night. It is a northern shower.

Why is the Quadrantid peak so short?

The stream is narrow and Earth passes through it at a steep angle. NASA gives the peak as a few hours. Wikipedia counts about eight hours above half the maximum rate, and the IMO's radio data suggest it is wider than the usual four hours.

What is the spiritual meaning of the Quadrantids?

Symbolically, it is the first meteor shower of the year and falls under a Capricorn Sun, which makes it a natural moment for a practical, short-term commitment. That is a reflective reading, not a prediction.
