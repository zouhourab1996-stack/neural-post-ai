How to Spot a Fake The Last Wish Review

You can use this checklist on any manifestation product, not only this one.

  1. Check the guarantee number. The seller's terms say 60 days. If a page promises 365 days, it did not read the terms.
  2. Look for proof of purchase. A real buyer can show a receipt (with personal details hidden), the download page, or the length of the audio file. Most "reviews" show only the sales-page images.
  3. Look for any downside. If a review lists no real cons and ends every section with a buy button, it is an ad.
  4. Check the byline. "Clinical Research Team" or an invented "MD" on a manifestation audio is a red flag.
  5. Watch for urgency copied from the sales page. "Today only," "59% off," and "limited window" are the seller's words, repeated.
  6. Check where it is hosted. A PDF on a government or charity site, a forum about data recovery, or a notes app is not a review site.
  7. Check the dates. Spam pages often carry "updated today" stamps that change every visit.

The Claims vs the Evidence

The sales page makes three big claims. Here is how each one holds up.

"Backed by research from Harvard and University of Michigan"

The page gives no study title, author, or link. Without a citation, there is nothing to check. Universities publish thousands of papers on sleep, meditation and the brain. That does not mean any of them tested this recording.

"Pineal gland-activating" and "decalcification"

The pineal gland is real. Its known job is making melatonin, the hormone that helps run your sleep cycle. Calcium deposits in the gland are common and increase with age. Researchers are still studying whether they affect sleep or health. We found no study showing that an audio track "activates" the pineal gland, and none linking the gland to money.

"Brainwave" audio

Many programs like this use binaural beats: two slightly different tones in each ear. The science is mixed:

  • A 2019 meta-analysis in Psychological Research (22 studies) found a medium average effect of binaural beats on anxiety, memory, attention and pain.
  • A 2023 systematic review in PLOS ONE looked at 14 brain-scan (EEG) studies. Fewer than half supported the idea that the beats "entrain" brainwaves. Most results were at least partly inconsistent.

So a calm audio track might help some people relax or focus for a while. That is a long way from "attract wealth and abundance." Relaxation is plausible. Money from listening is not supported by any evidence we found.
