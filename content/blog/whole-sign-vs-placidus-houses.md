:::tldr The key difference
- **Whole Sign houses** make the entire rising sign the first house, then assign one complete sign to each house.
- **Placidus** divides the chart using time-based arcs, so house sizes can be unequal.
- **Equal houses** begin at the exact Ascendant degree and then advance in twelve 30-degree steps.
- Switching systems can move a planet to a different house, but it does not change that planet's zodiac sign or degree when all other chart settings stay the same.
:::

Two astrology websites can calculate the same birth date, time and place yet show a planet in different houses. That does not automatically mean one chart is broken. The websites may be using different **house systems**: different ways of drawing the twelve house boundaries around the same birth chart.

The most familiar comparison is Whole Sign versus Placidus, although Equal houses are worth understanding too. This guide explains the geometry in everyday language, shows why the same planet can land in two different houses, and gives you a fair way to compare charts without changing several settings at once.

## What does a house system actually do?

A natal chart has twelve houses. In astrology, each house is used as a symbolic area of life: the first is associated with identity and presence, the second with resources, the seventh with one-to-one partnerships, the tenth with public roles and work, and so on.

A **house system** is the rule a chart calculator uses to mark where one house ends and the next begins. It is not the same setting as the zodiac. Tropical versus sidereal describes the zodiac framework; Whole Sign versus Placidus describes how the houses are divided. You can change one without deliberately changing the other.

The house boundaries depend on the birth moment and location. That is why a reliable birth time and birthplace matter so much when you want to compare house placements. Our [step-by-step natal chart guide](/blog/natal-chart-reading-guide-2026/) explains the Ascendant, houses and other chart components in context.

## Whole Sign, Placidus and Equal: the quick comparison

| System | Where the first house begins | How the other houses are drawn | Typical result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole Sign | At 0° of the Ascendant's zodiac sign | Each following sign becomes the next complete house | Twelve equal sign-sized houses |
| Placidus | At the exact Ascendant degree | Uses divisions of the rising and setting time arcs | Unequal house widths |
| Equal | At the exact Ascendant degree | Adds 30° for every next house | Twelve equal 30° houses, offset from sign boundaries |

All three produce twelve houses, but they do not ask the same geometric question. The Swiss Ephemeris manual describes Placidus through divisions of semi-diurnal and semi-nocturnal arcs, while its Equal-house method starts from the Ascendant and steps forward in 30-degree intervals. It describes Whole Sign houses as beginning at the start of the Ascendant's sign, with each house covering a complete sign.

## How Whole Sign houses work

In Whole Sign houses, the sign containing the Ascendant becomes the entire first house. The next sign in zodiacal order becomes the second house, the following sign the third, and the sequence continues around the chart until all twelve signs have been assigned.

Imagine a chart with **18° Gemini rising**. Gemini is the first house from 0° to 30° Gemini. Cancer is the second house, Leo the third, Virgo the fourth, and so on. The Ascendant is still at 18° Gemini; it simply sits inside the first house rather than marking that house's starting boundary.

This is the detail that often surprises people who learned Placidus first. In Whole Sign houses, the first house begins before the exact Ascendant degree. A planet earlier in the rising sign can therefore be in the first house even though it is below the horizon in the chart's visual layout.

The Swiss Ephemeris manual notes that Whole Sign houses were used in Hellenistic astrology and remain in use in Hindu astrology; contemporary Western astrologers also use the method. The method is straightforward to map: once the rising sign is known, the sign sequence determines the house sequence. That consistency is one reason some astrologers prefer it for organising topics and techniques such as annual profections.

### What happens to the Midheaven?

The Midheaven (MC) is the ecliptic degree that intersects the local meridian at birth. It is a chart angle; it is not identical to the tenth house in every house system. In Whole Sign houses, the MC does not have to be the tenth-house cusp. Its degree can fall in a different whole-sign house, while the tenth house remains the tenth sign counted from the Ascendant.

That is not necessarily a software error. Some astrologers interpret the MC degree alongside the tenth house rather than treating the two as interchangeable. The distinction is discussed in the historical overview of house division by [The Astrology Podcast, episode 227](https://theastrologypodcast.com/transcripts/ep-227-transcript-origins-of-the-house-division-debate-in-ancient-astrology/).

## How Placidus houses work

Placidus is a quadrant house system. It starts with the chart's angles — the Ascendant, Descendant, Midheaven and IC — and calculates the intermediate house cusps using divisions of the relevant semi-diurnal and semi-nocturnal arcs. In plain language, the method is based on the time it takes parts of the zodiac to move through the visible and invisible halves of the sky.

Because the zodiac does not rise and culminate at a uniform rate at every latitude, the resulting houses are not necessarily 30° wide. One house may span a relatively small portion of the zodiac while another is much larger. The precise cusps depend on the birth time and geographic location.

In Placidus, the Ascendant is the first-house cusp and the Midheaven is the tenth-house cusp. A sign can be split across two houses, and at some latitudes a complete sign can sit inside a house without having a cusp of its own. Astrologers call that an interception. It does not mean the sign is missing or that a person lacks the qualities associated with it; it is a feature of the house geometry in that chart.

Placidus is a valid, long-used method within modern Western astrology, but it is not the only possible way to divide a chart. Its technical definition is set out in section 6.2.1 of the [Swiss Ephemeris documentation](https://www.astro.com/swisseph-download/doc/swisseph.pdf).

## How Equal houses work

Equal houses are also 30° wide, but they begin at the exact Ascendant degree rather than at 0° of the rising sign. If the Ascendant is 18° Gemini, the first house runs from 18° Gemini to 18° Cancer. The second runs from 18° Cancer to 18° Leo, and every following house adds another 30°.

That means Equal and Whole Sign houses are not interchangeable, even though both use twelve equal sections. Whole Sign follows the sign boundaries; Equal follows the exact Ascendant degree.

The Swiss Ephemeris manual lists both methods separately. In a conversation on house systems, astrologers Carole Taylor and Chris Brennan also describe Equal houses as 30-degree divisions measured from the Ascendant, while noting that the MC/IC axis can fall away from the tenth/fourth house cusps in this system. See [The Astrology Podcast, episode 266](https://theastrologypodcast.com/transcripts/tap-ep-266-transcript-aspect-patterns-in-astrology-with-carole-taylor/).

## One planet, two house placements: a worked example

Return to the hypothetical chart with **18° Gemini rising** and imagine a planet at **7° Cancer**. Keep the same tropical zodiac and birth data; change only the house system.

- **Whole Sign:** Gemini is the first house and Cancer is the second, so the planet is in the second house.
- **Equal:** the first house runs from 18° Gemini to 18° Cancer. Since 7° Cancer is before the second-house boundary at 18° Cancer, the planet remains in the first house.
- **Placidus:** the placement depends on the calculated Placidus second-house cusp for the actual birth time and location. Without those inputs, it would be misleading to guess the planet's house.

This example is about the arithmetic of the systems, not a real person's chart. It shows why a planet can move from the first to the second house when you change the house system, even though its zodiac longitude is unchanged.

## What changes — and what does not?

When you change only the house system in a chart calculator, the **house boundaries and house placements** can change. That can also change which sign appears on a particular cusp in a quadrant system, and which house an astrologer uses when interpreting a planet.

The following details do not change merely because you selected a different house system:

- The planetary zodiac signs and longitudes, provided you keep the same zodiac framework and calculation settings.
- The birth date, exact birth time and geographic coordinates entered into the calculator.
- The Ascendant and Midheaven degrees calculated from those same birth details.
- The angular distance between planets and therefore the chart's planetary aspects.

There is an important distinction: the Ascendant degree stays the same, but its role in the house grid differs. In Placidus and Equal houses it marks the start of the first house. In Whole Sign houses it is a sensitive point within the first house. The MC degree also stays the same, but it is not necessarily the tenth-house cusp in Whole Sign or Equal houses.

## A high-latitude warning for Placidus charts

Birthplace latitude can expose a technical limitation that is easy to miss. Beyond the polar circles, some Placidus and Koch house cusps cannot be calculated in the usual way. The Swiss Ephemeris manual explains that its house calculation may switch to Porphyry houses in those cases and return a warning.

If you were born in a far-northern or far-southern location, read the chart calculator's notice rather than assuming the label on the settings menu tells the whole story. A displayed chart may use a fallback method for some or all house cusps. A calculator should disclose that clearly.

For an uncertain or unusual chart, compare the stated house method with the actual output and consult the software's documentation. Do not interpret a silent fallback as proof that Placidus was calculated normally.

## How to compare house systems in your own chart

A useful comparison changes one variable at a time. Try this sequence:

1. **Use the best birth data available.** Check the recorded time, birthplace and time-zone history. A rounded or uncertain birth time can affect the Ascendant and house boundaries.
2. **Choose one chart calculator.** Keep the location, date, time, zodiac type, ayanamsa if applicable, and other settings unchanged.
3. **Save the first chart.** Note the house system and record the house placement of the Sun, Moon, chart ruler and any planet close to a cusp.
4. **Switch only the house system.** Compare Whole Sign, Placidus and Equal if all three are available.
5. **Write down what moved.** Focus on the planets that changed houses and on the location of the MC. Do not assume a changed house means a changed zodiac sign.
6. **Read each version consistently.** If a book, astrologer or technique specifies a house system, use that system for the relevant interpretation rather than mixing boundaries from several charts.

If you do not know your exact birth time, do not treat a house comparison as certain. The Ascendant and house cusps depend on the time and place. ## Which house system should you use?

There is no universally accepted winner, and house systems have been debated within astrology for centuries. The choice is best treated as a method decision rather than a search for the chart that tells the most flattering story.

- **Choose Whole Sign** when you want clear sign-to-house mapping and a consistent framework for sign-based techniques such as annual profections. It is also easy to explain and compare.
- **Choose Placidus** when you want to work within a quadrant system based on time arcs, or when you are following a book, teacher or consultation that specifically uses Placidus.
- **Choose Equal** when you want twelve identical 30° houses measured from the exact Ascendant degree, but do not want house boundaries locked to the zodiac sign boundaries.

If you are learning, start with one system and understand its rules before comparing another. If you are studying a traditional technique, use the house method that technique calls for. If you consult different astrologers, ask which system they use and keep that context attached to their reading.

The important thing is to avoid switching methods simply because a planet moves into a house that sounds more appealing. A consistent method makes it easier to see what the chart actually says within that system.
