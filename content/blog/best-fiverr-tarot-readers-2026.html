The question is 80% of the answer

Readers can only work with the frame you hand them. Compare:

  • Weak: "Will I find love this year?" — invites a hopeful script.
  • Strong: "I keep choosing partners who pull away after a few months. What does the reading say I'm not seeing about my own pattern?" — invites real interpretation.

Strong questions name a situation, a tension, and a decision. They give the reader something to read against. If you only have a weak question, say so and ask for a general reading — a good reader will tell you honestly that there is not much to work with.

How to vet a tarot seller in five minutes

  • The gig names the spread and the deck. Rider-Waite, Thoth, Marseille — each reads differently, and a reader who names theirs knows theirs.
  • Reviews mention cards, not vibes. "She pulled the Tower for my job situation and explained the timing" is a real review.
  • Delivery is described concretely. Length in minutes, video or PDF, turnaround time, whether you submit questions in advance.
  • They answer a pre-order question specifically. Ask: "Do you work with a spread I choose, or your own?"
  • No upsell ladder in the description. If Standard and Premium are mostly "removes a block" or "cleanses energy", read our red flags guide before paying anything.

Can tarot predict the future?

Readers differ, and so do clients. The practical framing most good readers use is not prediction but perspective: cards as a structured way to look at a situation you are too close to see. Treat any claim of guaranteed prediction as a red flag.

How many cards do I actually need?

Three, for most questions. A well-read three-card spread answers more than a rushed ten-card one. Go bigger only when the situation genuinely has multiple moving parts.

Do I need to be present for the reading?

No, and you usually do not need to be online. Most Fiverr readers ask for your question in advance and deliver a recording or written reading — which is also better value, since you keep the file.

Does the deck matter?

Somewhat. Rider-Waite-Smith imagery is the most widely taught, so interpretations tend to be more consistent. Any deck works if the reader knows it well — the question to ask is which deck they use and why.

What if the reading is negative?

A reader who only ever says pleasant things is not reading, they are performing. Cards like the Tower or Death are routinely about endings that make room for something — ask the reader to explain the card in context rather than taking a single image literally.

Card count: what three cards give you that ten do not

More cards is not more reading. A three-card spread forces the reader to commit to a shape — situation, obstacle, outcome — and leaves nowhere to hide. A ten-card spread gives the reader room to narrate, which is exactly why some sellers prefer it: with ten cards, something will always seem to fit.

The practical difference is in what you walk away with. Three cards gives you a sentence you can act on. Ten gives you a story. If you have a specific decision to make, buy three cards. If you want to sit with a situation and hear it described from several angles, buy the bigger spread and expect reflection rather than an answer.

Deck type, and whether it should matter to you

Most marketplace readers work with a Rider-Waite-Smith deck or a close variant, and that is a reasonable default: its imagery is the most widely taught, so the interpretations are the ones you can look up afterwards and check. Some readers use Thoth, Marseille, or oracle decks with no fixed structure at all.

Deck choice is not a quality signal, but it is a checkability signal. If you want to verify what a card meant, a standard deck gives you something to verify against. Ask which deck they read with if the gig does not say — a reader who cannot name their own deck is probably working from a phone app.

Video, written, or chat: which format suits which question

Each format has a genuine use. A written reading gives you a document to return to, which matters for anything you want to think about for weeks — and it is usually the cheapest per word. A video session lets you interrupt and ask what a card meant, which matters when the reading is about something you are still figuring out. Chat is fast and inexpensive but loses tone, which is a real loss when the subject is emotional.

Match the format to the half-life of the question. A decision you will make this week does not need a recorded session. Something you will be sitting with for months is worth having in writing.

What a tarot reading cannot do

Stating the limits protects you from the sellers who ignore them. Tarot cannot diagnose a medical condition, predict a death, tell you what another person is thinking, or give you a date on which something will happen. It is a structured prompt for thinking about a situation, and a skilled reader is someone who uses the cards to ask you better questions than you were asking yourself.

Any reading that moves from “the cards suggest” to “this will happen on” has left the method behind. And any reading that finds a blockage, curse, or negative attachment — and offers to clear it for an extra fee — is the pattern consumer-protection guidance on psychic fraud warns about specifically.

How to judge the reading you received

Two tests, and the second matters more. First, the mechanical test: does the reader identify the cards and say what each meant? If they name cards you can look up, you can check the work. Second, the usefulness test: a week later, has it changed anything you did or decided?

Be careful with the feeling of accuracy. A reading feels accurate when it tells you something you already half-knew, and it is easy to credit the cards for what you brought with you. That is not a criticism of tarot — it is the honest description of what a good session is. Judge it on whether it organised your thinking, not on whether it felt uncanny.
